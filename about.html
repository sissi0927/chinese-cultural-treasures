Project Overview

Chinese Cultural Treasures

This Web 3D application presents three iconic Chinese cultural objects — a round fan (tuánshàn), a stylised giant panda, and a classical hairpin (fāzān) — through an interactive 3D viewer built with Three.js, HTML5, CSS3, and JavaScript. Each object was modelled in Autodesk Maya and loaded into the browser, where the user can rotate, inspect, toggle wireframe, adjust lighting, and switch camera angles.

The visual identity draws on the earthy glaze palette of Tang Sancai ceramics — amber, ceramic green, cream, and deep brown — creating a warm, culturally rooted aesthetic that frames the objects without competing with them.

Design Direction

Curatorial Intent

The central aim is to present Chinese material culture not merely as decoration but as a series of layered symbolic objects, each carrying its own historical and aesthetic meaning. The interface is designed to support storytelling: the right-hand panel updates with cultural context as the user switches models, while the controls section offers genuine 3D interaction rather than passive display.

The three objects were chosen deliberately to represent different registers of Chinese cultural life — refined decorative art, national natural symbolism, and classical personal adornment — together forming a small but coherent cultural portrait.

Cultural Framework

Objects as Cultural Carriers

In Chinese cultural tradition, everyday objects are rarely purely functional. The fan conveys a social and aesthetic identity; the hairpin signals status, occasion, and femininity; even the panda — though a living animal — has been constructed through decades of diplomacy and visual culture into a potent national symbol. This exhibition treats each object as a carrier of cultural meaning, using both the 3D form and the descriptive text to surface those layers.

This approach is informed by material culture studies, which argues that objects embody social relations, aesthetic values, and historical memory in ways that direct representation cannot.

Object Selection Rationale

Why These Three?

The three objects occupy distinct cultural registers. The round fan belongs to the domain of cultivated leisure and decorative art. The giant panda operates in the register of national identity and diplomatic soft power. The hairpin is an object of intimate personal adornment tied to ceremony and classical femininity. Together, they represent public culture, natural identity, and private life — a deliberately varied set of entry points into Chinese visual and material culture.

3D Model Documentation

The Three Cultural Objects

01 — Chinese Round Fan (团扇 Tuánshàn)

The round fan has a history stretching back to the Han Dynasty (206 BCE – 220 CE), where it was used by members of the imperial court as both a practical cooling device and a signifier of elegance and refinement. By the Tang and Song periods, the round fan had become a canvas for painting and calligraphy — artists including Su Shi and Ma Yuan painted directly onto fan surfaces, creating miniature works of high cultural prestige.

The fan's circular form carries symbolic weight in Chinese aesthetics: the circle (yuán, 圆) represents completeness, harmony, and the unity of heaven and earth. The long handle adds a sense of poise and physical grace to the object. Fans appear frequently in classical Chinese painting, opera costume, and literary imagery as a marker of cultivated taste.

3D Design Notes: Modelled in Maya using a polygon disc for the fan face, a revolved CV curve for the handle, and a tassel constructed from extruded curves. The surface material uses a warm silk-like texture with painted motif detail. The symmetry and silhouette of the object make it an ideal subject for front-view and top-view camera exploration.

02 — Giant Panda (大熊猫 Dàxióngmāo)

The giant panda (Ailuropoda melanoleuca) is native to the mountain forests of central China, primarily Sichuan, Shaanxi, and Gansu provinces. Although pandas have been recorded in Chinese natural history texts for over two thousand years, their global cultural prominence is largely a product of the twentieth century. China's use of pandas as diplomatic gifts — most famously to the United States and the Soviet Union in the 1970s — established the animal as a symbol of Chinese goodwill and international identity.

Within the exhibition, the panda serves a specific curatorial function: it introduces warmth, approachability, and contemporary cultural recognition into a set otherwise dominated by historical decorative objects. It is also the only living subject in the collection, making it a bridge between natural heritage and cultural symbol-making.

3D Design Notes: Modelled in Maya using polygon primitives — spheres, cylinders, and capsule shapes — merged and smoothed to build the body, head, limbs, and ears. The characteristic black-and-white colour blocking is achieved through separate material assignments on each mesh region, keeping the geometry clean and the rendering efficient.

03 — Classical Hairpin (发簪 Fāzān)

The hairpin is one of the oldest forms of personal adornment in Chinese culture, with examples recovered from Neolithic archaeological sites. During the Tang Dynasty, hairpins reached an apex of material elaboration: court women wore multiple hairpins simultaneously, crafted from gold, silver, jade, tortoiseshell, and kingfisher feather inlay (diǎncuì, 点翠). The hairpin's form — a long pin with an ornamental head — created a visual accent point in elaborate court hairstyles that could signal dynasty loyalty, seasonal occasion, or marital status.

In classical Chinese poetry, the hairpin appears as a recurring motif of feminine beauty and romantic longing. In the Dream of the Red Chamber (Hóng Lóu Mèng), hairpins function as charged objects exchanged between characters, carrying emotional and symbolic weight beyond their material form.

3D Design Notes: Modelled in Maya using a CV curve revolved into a surface for the tapered pin shaft, with the ornamental head built from boolean operations on polygon primitives. A metallic material with specular highlights is applied to simulate gold surface quality. It is the most geometrically intricate of the three models, with the ornamental head providing significant detail for close-up and side-view inspection.

3D Integration

Three.js Scene Architecture

Each model is exported from Maya as a GLB file and loaded via the Three.js GLTFLoader. A shared scene is maintained across model switches; a pivot Group wrapper centres each model at the world origin, ensuring self-rotation rather than orbital rotation. A four-light rig (ambient, key, fill, rim) is used when Light is ON; Light OFF uses high-intensity ambient only for flat natural display.

Camera presets (front, side, top) reposition the PerspectiveCamera and call OrbitControls.update(). Wireframe mode traverses all meshes and toggles material.wireframe. Two animation modes are available: continuous Y-axis rotation and a float animation (sinusoidal Y oscillation with slow drift), both driven by requestAnimationFrame.

Interface Design

Layout and Interaction Logic

The homepage is divided into four primary zones: a 3D viewer panel (left), a cultural information panel (right), a controls strip, and a model card gallery below. When the user selects a new model — either via the control buttons or the zone cards — the JavaScript updates the 3D viewer, swaps the text content in the information panel, and updates the status strip. This content-swapping approach means the page never reloads; all state changes are driven by event listeners.

Responsive Behaviour

Breakpoints and Adaptations

The layout uses CSS Grid with defined breakpoints at 1100 px and 900 px. At 1100 px, multi-column grids in the controls and reference sections collapse to two or one column. At 900 px, the main hero layout shifts from a two-column split to a single stacked column, and the header navigation adjusts to wrap beneath the brand block. All interactive buttons remain accessible at mobile scale.

Interaction Design

Control Features and Their Purpose

Model Switch

Three square buttons labelled Fan, Panda, and Hairpin allow the user to load each model into the viewer. On selection, the 3D object, descriptive text, meta information, and status display all update simultaneously via JavaScript DOM manipulation. The active model button is highlighted in green to confirm the current selection.

Animation Controls

Rotate triggers continuous Y-axis self-rotation. Float activates a sinusoidal bobbing animation — the model gently rises and falls with a slow Y-axis drift and subtle Z tilt, evoking the feeling of a cultural artefact suspended in display space. Stop pauses all animation. Reset returns position, rotation, wireframe, and lighting to their default states.

Wireframe Toggle

The Wireframe button toggles the material's wireframe property on all meshes of the active model. This reveals the underlying geometry — the number of polygon faces, edge loops, and structural decisions made during modelling. It is an educational feature that makes the construction of the 3D object visible.

Light Control

A secondary directional light can be switched on or off to alter the shading of the active model. With the light off, a single ambient and directional source is used. With the light on, a second fill light from the opposite side reduces shadow depth and reveals surface detail on the darker faces of the mesh.

Camera Presets

Front, Side, and Top buttons reposition the PerspectiveCamera to three preset positions around the model. Each view is paired with an appropriate look-at target. This allows structured comparison of the model's profile from defined angles rather than only freeform orbit. OrbitControls remain active after each camera jump, so the user can continue adjusting from the new position.

Music Control

A Music button in the Display section toggles background ambient audio on and off. The audio is a looping track that plays at a reduced volume (0.4) to complement the exhibition atmosphere without distracting from the 3D content. Playback state is reflected in the status strip and respects browser autoplay policies — audio only begins after an explicit user gesture.

Status Strip

A persistent status row below the viewer displays the currently active model name, wireframe state (On / Off), light state (On / Off), camera position (Front / Side / Top), and music playback state (On / Off). This gives users a clear readout of all interaction states without requiring them to read button colours or positions.

Development Process

Build Sequence

Development followed a structured sequence: HTML structure and CSS layout were established first as a static design, then JavaScript control logic was added, then the Three.js scene was integrated into the viewer container, and finally the Maya models were exported and loaded. This order ensured the interface remained testable and legible at every stage, independent of the 3D assets.

Modelling Workflow

Maya Pipeline

All three objects were created in Autodesk Maya using polygon modelling techniques. The fan uses a disc base with edge loop refinements and a lathe-modified handle. The panda uses merged polygon primitives shaped with the sculpt deformer. The hairpin uses a CV curve revolved into a surface for the pin shaft with a boolean-refined ornamental head. Models were exported as GLB files with embedded materials for efficient browser loading.

Usability

Interface Clarity

Button labels are explicit and consistent. Active states use a distinct green colour to confirm selection. The status strip below the viewer provides a real-time summary of all toggleable states. Section headings use a small-caps eyebrow label above each title to clarify the function of each panel. Navigation between Home and About is persistent in the header.

Accessibility

Accessibility Considerations

Text contrast is maintained above WCAG AA ratios throughout. Navigation links use visible active states. All section headings follow a logical H1 → H2 → H3 document hierarchy. Image elements carry descriptive alt attributes. Interactive buttons use semantic <button> elements to ensure keyboard and screen reader accessibility. Further improvements could include ARIA live regions for the model status updates.

Software Architecture

MVC Design Pattern

Model (Data Layer)

The modelData object in js/main.js acts as the application's data model. It stores all per-object metadata — name, file path, theme, material description, cultural description, context, and concept text — as a structured JavaScript object keyed by model identifier ("fan", "panda", "hairpin"). The Three.js scene graph, loaded pivot groups, and light/state variables also form part of the model layer, representing the current 3D application state.

View (Presentation Layer)

The View is split across two files. index.html defines the static DOM structure — the viewer panel, side-info panel, control cards, zone cards, reference gallery, and status strip — using semantic HTML5 elements. css/style.css handles all visual presentation through CSS custom properties, Grid layout, backdrop blur, and responsive breakpoints, with no inline styles on dynamic elements. The Three.js WebGLRenderer canvas is also a View component, rendering the 3D scene into #model-container.

Controller (Logic Layer)

js/main.js is the Controller. It attaches event listeners to all buttons and zone cards, calls switchToModel() to load and display the correct GLB, calls updateUI() to push new data from the Model into the View's DOM elements, and manages all 3D scene state (wireframe, lights, camera, animation). The animate() loop serves as the real-time controller for the rendering pipeline and animation state machine.

Deeper Understanding

Advanced Technical Features

The following three features extend the application beyond the standard lab scope and demonstrate deeper understanding of web 3D production.

Feature 1 — IBL via PMREMGenerator

Image-Based Lighting (IBL) is implemented using Three.js PMREMGenerator and RoomEnvironment. The generator converts an analytic room environment into a prefiltered radiance environment map (PMREM), which is assigned to scene.environment. All MeshStandardMaterial instances in the loaded GLB files automatically sample this map for physically correct ambient reflections — particularly visible on the metallic hairpin surface and the fan's wooden trim. This replaces the traditional point-light-only approach with a physically based lighting model that responds to material roughness and metalness values baked in Maya.

Feature 2 — Post-Processing Bloom (UnrealBloomPass)

A post-processing pipeline is constructed using Three.js EffectComposer, replacing the standard renderer.render() call with a multi-pass pipeline: RenderPass → UnrealBloomPass → OutputPass. The UnrealBloomPass applies physically-inspired glow to pixels exceeding a luminance threshold, simulating lens bloom on bright specular highlights. Bloom parameters are tuned per model: the metallic hairpin uses a higher strength (0.40) and lower threshold (0.90) to produce visible golden glow on highlight areas; the matte fan and panda use near-invisible settings (strength 0.08, threshold 0.98) to avoid overexposure of their diffuse surfaces. The OutputPass handles final sRGB colour space conversion and tone mapping output.

Feature 3 — Float Animation (Story-Driven Motion)

Beyond simple object rotation, a float animation is implemented that uses a THREE.Clock to drive sinusoidal position and rotation values per frame. The Y position oscillates as sin(t × 1.2) × 0.12 units, creating a gentle bobbing motion. A secondary slow Y-axis rotation (0.004 rad/frame) and a Z-axis tilt (sin(t × 0.7) × 0.04 rad) are overlaid to produce a compound motion that suggests the object is suspended in an exhibition space. This animation mode is narratively justified: it evokes the presentation of cultural artefacts in a curated display context, going beyond the mechanical rotation covered in the lab tutorials. Autodesk Maya was also used for all 3D modelling — a tool outside the lab curriculum — demonstrating further depth of practice.

Statement of Originality

Original Work Declaration

All 3D models presented in this application — the Chinese round fan, the giant panda, and the classical hairpin — were created from scratch in Autodesk Maya as original geometry. The HTML structure, CSS design system, and JavaScript interaction logic were written independently for this project. The cultural text content is original writing informed by the academic and historical sources listed in the References section below. No pre-made templates, third-party UI kits, or AI-generated code were used in the construction of this site.
